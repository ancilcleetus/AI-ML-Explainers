1
What is Model Deployment? — Interactive Explainer ↗
The model lifecycle from a fresh checkpoint to a production endpoint — train → optimize → export → serve → monitor → maintain — illustrated as a flow diagram. The training-vs-inference asymmetry made concrete: training is expensive but one-time, inference is cheap per call but compounds over millions of requests, and they often want different hardware (training likes raw FLOPS, inference often prefers memory bandwidth and INT8 support). Why deployment is its own discipline rather than a coda to training: the engineering constraints are different, the metrics are different, and the failure modes are different. Why "it works in my notebook" almost never translates to production. A quick tour of what the rest of the roadmap covers and the order to read it in.

The Four Deployment Constraints — Interactive Explainer ↗
Every deployment decision trades off four axes: latency (how long a single request takes), throughput (how many requests per second), memory (model size in RAM and on disk), and accuracy (how well it predicts). 5
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) — Interactive Explainer ↗
The "no retraining required" approach — take a trained FP32 model, calibrate it on a small unlabeled validation set, output an INT8 model. The calibration step explained: histograms of activations are collected at each layer to find the optimal scale range. Per-tensor vs per-channel quantization compared on a real ResNet-18 layer (callback to CV #14 MobileNet's pursuit of efficiency) — per-channel typically recovers 0.5–1% accuracy at minimal cost. When PTQ is enough — most CNNs at INT8 lose <1% top-1 accuracy. When PTQ fails — some attention layers and BatchNorm-fused convs need help. PyTorch reference: torch.ao.quantization.quantize_dynamic and the static-quantization workflow with a runnable example.

6
Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) — Interactive Explainer ↗
When PTQ loses too much accuracy, you simulate quantization during training so the model can adapt. Fake quantization nodes inserted into the forward pass; the straight-through estimator letting gradients flow through round() during the backward pass. An interactive side-by-side: same model, same dataset, PTQ vs QAT — watch the val-accuracy gap close as QAT trains. When QAT pays off (low-bit quantization, INT4 weight-only for LLMs, edge deployment of object detectors). The training-cost overhead (typically 10–30% slower than FP32 training). PyTorch's torch.ao.quantization QAT workflow walked through end to end. Brief mention of Brevitas and the more advanced QAT libraries.

Pruning
7
Pruning — Interactive Explainer ↗
Removing weights that don't matter much. Unstructured pruning (zeroing individual weights, producing a sparse matrix) vs structured pruning (zeroing entire channels or filters, producing a smaller dense matrix). Magnitude-based pruning explained: rank weights by absolute value, zero the bottom k%. An interactive demo: prune a real Conv layer at different sparsity levels, see the accuracy-vs-size Pareto curve. The iterative prune-fine-tune loop that recovers most of the lost accuracy. The lottery ticket hypothesis as intuition (small subnetworks were always good; pruning just reveals them). Why pruning often underperforms quantization in real deployment — sparse compute is poorly supported on most accelerators outside NVIDIA's 2:4 structured sparsity on Ampere+ GPUs. When pruning still pays off: extreme model compression for storage, latency wins on hardware with sparse-aware kernels.

8
Knowledge Distillation — Interactive Explainer ↗
Compressing a big teacher model into a smaller student that mimics it. The teacher-student paradigm: forward-pass the same input through both, train the student to match the teacher's outputs. Soft labels (the teacher's full probability distribution, not just argmax) carry far more information than hard labels — explained with a worked example showing why "cat 0.82, dog 0.15, fox 0.03" teaches the student more than just "cat". Temperature in softmax (callback to DL #6 Softmax) — why softening the teacher's distribution helps the student learn. KL divergence as the distillation loss. The relationship to CV #30 DeiT's distillation token. Distilling LLMs into smaller LLMs as a preview of Cat 6 (DistilBERT, the modern small-LLM family). When distillation beats quantization (very-different student architectures, multi-task student, accuracy floor matters).

From PyTorch eager mode to graphs
9
TorchScript & torch.compile — Interactive Explainer ↗
Why PyTorch's default "eager mode" (build the graph as Python runs) is bad for production — every forward pass pays Python interpreter overhead, and you can't ship a Python interpreter to a phone. The fix: capture the model as a static graph. TorchScript via torch.jit.script (parses Python source, supports control flow) vs torch.jit.trace (records one execution, fast but can't handle data-dependent branches) with their gotchas — an interactive flowchart for picking between them. The PyTorch 2.x compile story: torch.compile uses TorchDynamo to capture the graph, AOT autograd to handle the backward, and Inductor as the code generator. What you actually get (typically 1.3–2× speedup on training, similar on inference). When compile fails (unsupported ops, dynamic shapes, custom CUDA kernels) and the fallback graph-break behavior.

ONNX — The Universal Format — Interactive Explainer ↗
ONNX as the lingua franca between training frameworks and inference engines. The opset as a contract — each ONNX version defines a fixed set of operators with fixed semantics; exporters target an opset, importers support a range. Exporting from PyTorch with torch.onnx.export walked through end to end. Numerical validation — the canonical workflow of running the same input through PyTorch eager and ONNX Runtime and asserting agreement to within 1e-5. Debugging "operator not supported" errors (typically: a Python op had no ONNX equivalent in your opset; the fix is usually to bump the opset or rewrite that block). What gets baked into the ONNX file (weights, graph topology, input/output names and shapes). The portability promise — same ONNX file runs on CPU via ONNX Runtime, on NVIDIA via TensorRT, on Apple via Core ML Tools, on edge via TFLite. PyTorch reference + a complete export-and-verify recipe.

12
Edge & Mobile Hardware — Interactive Explainer ↗
The hardware you'll actually deploy to. Apple Neural Engine (ANE) — 17–35 TOPS at 1–3W on modern iPhones, INT8 native, accessed via Core ML. Snapdragon Hexagon NPU on Android — comparable specs, accessed via TFLite NNAPI delegate. NVIDIA Jetson Orin family — full CUDA stack, GPU + DLA accelerators, the "server-class edge" tier. Google Coral Edge TPU — INT8-only, 4 TOPS at 2W, USB or M.2 form factors. Raspberry Pi 5 with the AI HAT+ (Hailo-8L) — modern hobbyist edge. A specs table with TOPS, power draw, RAM, typical price, and what software stacks each one supports. The crucial gotcha: most edge devices are memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, which means FLOPs alone don't predict speed — depthwise separable convs (callback to CV #14 MobileNet) help with FLOPs but not always with memory bandwidth, while quantization (Cat 2) helps with both.

17
Batching, Concurrency, and GPU Utilization — Interactive Explainer ↗
Why GPU utilization is the metric that pays the bills, and why most production deployments idle their GPUs 80% of the time. Static batching — collect N requests, run one big forward pass, return all results; great when traffic is steady, bad when it's bursty. Dynamic batching — collect requests for up-to-W milliseconds or until batch-size N, whichever comes first; the standard Triton/TorchServe technique. Continuous batching — the LLM-specific innovation (deeper coverage in Cat 6 #21) where sequences of different lengths share a batch. Request queueing and backpressure — what happens when traffic exceeds throughput, how to shed load gracefully rather than degrade everyone's latency. Concurrency in Python — why GIL is mostly irrelevant for inference (the work happens in C++ kernels), but matters for preprocessing pipelines. An interactive simulator: pick traffic pattern + batching strategy, watch GPU utilization and P99 latency over time.

Why LLM inference is different
18
LLM Inference Fundamentals — Interactive Explainer ↗
Why everything in Cat 5 needs special treatment for autoregressive models. The two phases — prefill (process the prompt in parallel, compute-bound) vs decode (generate one token at a time, memory-bound). Why decode is the bottleneck — every token depends on the previous one, so you can't parallelize across the sequence; latency is dominated by memory bandwidth (load the KV cache from HBM, do one forward pass, repeat). Per-token latency vs throughput as the two LLM-specific metrics. The KV cache as the central concept — what gets cached, why it gets cached, how it grows linearly with sequence length and number of concurrent requests. An animated visualization of token-by-token autoregressive generation alongside the KV cache filling up. Why all the LLM-specific tricks in this category exist.

Optimizations specific to LLMs
19
KV Cache, FlashAttention & PagedAttention — Interactive Explainer ↗
The three innovations that made modern LLM serving possible. KV cache explained from scratch — when generating token N, you've already done all the attention math for tokens 1 through N-1; caching K and V across those tokens lets every new token compute attention in O(N) instead of O(N²). FlashAttention as an IO-aware reformulation — instead of materialising the full N×N attention matrix in HBM, tile it and keep partial results in SRAM; same math, 4–8× faster, much less memory. PagedAttention (vLLM's innovation, 2023) as memory paging applied to the KV cache — fixed-size pages can be allocated and freed independently per sequence, eliminating the fragmentation that limited max-concurrent-users in earlier systems. An interactive memory-allocation visualiser showing naive contiguous KV cache (lots of fragmentation, 30% wasted) vs PagedAttention (near-zero waste). The practical implications — modern LLM servers handle 10× more concurrent users than 2022-era servers at the same hardware budget.

20
LLM Quantization — GPTQ, AWQ, GGUF — Interactive Explainer ↗
Why LLM quantization is different from CNN quantization. Weight-only quantization — for LLMs, weights dominate memory but activations are small, so quantize weights to INT4/INT3, leave activations FP16. The math works out because: (a) the inference is memory-bandwidth-bound, so loading 4-bit weights gives nearly 4× speedup; (b) weights have nicer numerical distributions than activations. GPTQ — calibration-based weight quantization that minimises layer-wise output error via second-order optimization; produces INT4 weights with <1 point perplexity loss. AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) — preserves the weight rows that correspond to high-magnitude activations at higher precision. GGUF — llama.cpp's binary file format, with quantization levels like Q4_K_M and Q5_K_S that you'll encounter on Hugging Face. An interactive demo: pick a quantization level (FP16 / INT8 / INT4 / INT3) and see the size-vs-perplexity-vs-tokens-per-second trade-off on a small open-weights LLM.

21
Speculative Decoding & Continuous Batching — Interactive Explainer ↗
Two throughput multipliers that don't trade off accuracy. Speculative decoding — a small "draft" model generates k tokens fast; the big target model verifies all k in a single parallel forward pass and accepts the prefix that matches its own distribution. When the draft model is fast and accurate (typically 70%+ acceptance), you get 2–3× wall-clock speedup with zero quality loss. The math of acceptance probability and expected speedup walked through. Continuous batching (vLLM, SGLang) — instead of waiting for the slowest sequence in a batch to finish before starting new ones, swap finished sequences out of the batch as they end and slot new ones in. This is what "continuous" means — the batch is always full. The pair of innovations together transformed LLM serving economics around 2023–2024. When each technique pays off and when it doesn't (very short prompts barely benefit; very long prompts benefit most).

24
Data Drift & Model Drift — Interactive Explainer ↗
The canonical "94% accuracy in eval, 71% in production after three months" problem. Three flavours of drift: input drift (the distribution of your inputs changes — users upload different kinds of photos), label drift (the relationship between inputs and labels changes — fraud patterns evolve), output drift (your model's prediction distribution shifts even when inputs look stable). Detection techniques — Population Stability Index (PSI), Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, KL divergence between reference and live distributions. An interactive visualisation: a reference distribution and a sliding live distribution; watch PSI tick up as drift increases. When to retrain (rules of thumb based on drift magnitude vs business impact). Shadow deployment — run the new model in parallel with the old, log both, only switch when shadow performance validates. A/B testing for safe model rollouts. Brief mention of canary deployments.

Training at scale
27
Distributed Training — Data Parallel, FSDP & ZeRO — Interactive Explainer ↗
Survey of how modern models train on more than one GPU. The single-GPU memory wall — a 7B-parameter model in FP16 needs ~14GB for weights, ~14GB for gradients, ~28GB+ for Adam optimizer states (1st and 2nd moments), plus activations — easily 100GB+ during training. Data Parallelism (DP, DDP) — every GPU holds a full model copy and processes a different mini-batch; gradients are all-reduced after each backward pass. The visual: a worked example showing gradient sync via ring all-reduce across 4 GPUs. The memory problem with naïve DP — every GPU holds a full model copy, so you can't train a model that doesn't fit on one GPU. The ZeRO progression (DeepSpeed's contribution) — ZeRO-1 shards optimizer states across GPUs, ZeRO-2 also shards gradients, ZeRO-3 / FSDP shards parameters too (only assemble what you need for each forward step, gather on demand). A memory-budget diagram showing each stage's footprint per GPU. Brief mention of tensor parallelism (split a single layer across GPUs) and pipeline parallelism (different GPUs handle different layers). Forward pointer: this is the only training-side explainer in Deployment — for the full training-infrastructure story, a future "Scaling Training" roadmap will go deep on Megatron-LM, DeepSpeed, and the 3D parallelism story.

28
Gradient Checkpointing & Memory-Efficient Training — Interactive Explainer ↗
Two more techniques for training big models on small GPUs. Why activations dominate training memory — for backprop you need to remember the activations from the forward pass at every layer; for a deep model these can easily 10× the weight memory. Gradient checkpointing as the time-vs-memory trade-off — instead of storing every layer's activations, store a few "checkpoints" along the network and recompute the in-between activations during the backward pass. A visual showing the layer-by-layer memory profile with and without checkpointing (~70% memory reduction typical, ~25% slower training). Mixed-precision training — fp16 / bf16 weights and activations halve the memory and double the throughput on modern GPUs; the loss-scaling trick that prevents underflow in the fp16 backward pass; bf16 as the modern default (same exponent range as fp32, less mantissa precision). Combined with FSDP (#27), these are how trillion-parameter models train at all. A brief mention of activation offloading to CPU as the extreme-memory-pressure option.

CI/CD for ML — Continuous Training & Deployment Pipelines — Interactive Explainer ↗
Why ML pipelines differ from software CI/CD. In software, the only inputs are code; in ML, the inputs are code and data and hyperparameters and the previous model — and data changes, models drift (callback to #24). The canonical pipeline diagram: new data arrives → data validation (schema checks, drift detection) → training run → eval on holdout → comparison-with-prod model → if improved, blessed and promoted to staging → automated tests against staging → deployed to production. Tools at survey depth — GitHub Actions for the simple case (great when you only retrain monthly), Kubeflow Pipelines for the Kubernetes-native case, Argo Workflows for the general-purpose orchestrator, Airflow as the venerable workflow tool, Prefect as the modern Python-native alternative. DVC for data versioning (git for datasets). Brief mention of feature stores (Feast, Tecton) for when many models share preprocessing. When to invest in CI/CD (production traffic with frequent retraining) vs when not to (research, prototypes, one-off models). Honest take: most teams under-invest in this until they have an incident.

Cloud deployment landscape
31
Cloud Deployment Platforms — A Survey — Interactive Explainer ↗
The four-tier landscape, organised by how much the platform does for you. Tier 1: raw IaaS (AWS EC2, GCP Compute Engine, Azure VMs) — you provision the machine, install CUDA, manage scaling. Maximum control, maximum ops burden. Tier 2: managed ML platforms (AWS SageMaker, GCP Vertex AI, Azure ML) — they orchestrate training jobs, endpoints, model versioning; you bring the model and config. Tier 3: serverless ML inference (Modal, Replicate, RunPod, Banana, Beam) — they handle scaling and GPU allocation; you bring a Python function. Cold-start costs apply. Tier 4: BYO-LLM and managed-LLM platforms (Together AI, Anyscale Endpoints, Fireworks, Groq) — they host the LLM, you call an API. Plus the API-only providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) at the far end. A decision flowchart based on (latency tolerance, cost predictability, ops budget, GPU availability, model size). The unwritten rule for most teams: start serverless to validate, graduate to managed when you outgrow it, only build raw if you have a specialised need. Honest caveat: pricing and exact features in this space date fast — focus on the structural differences, not specific dollar figures.
