From Counting to Learning

In the previous explainer we built N-gram models — language models that work by pure counting. They were elegant, but we found two flaws that no amount of patching could fix:

  • Sparsity — most word combinations are never seen, so they get probability zero. A trigram model with a 50,000-word vocabulary has 125 trillion possible triples; no corpus can ever fill that table.
  • No notion of similarity — to an N-gram model, “cat” and “dog” are unrelated symbols. Nothing learned about one transfers to the other.

Both flaws share a single root cause: N-grams memorize rather than understand. The fix is the central idea of this explainer — instead of counting symbols, we learn representations that capture what words actually mean. This launches the second era of language modeling.

Bengio's Breakthrough: Words as Vectors

In 2003, Yoshua Bengio and colleagues published “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model” with a deceptively simple core idea: represent each word as a dense vector of real numbers (an embedding), and use a neural network to predict the next word from those embeddings.

The phone book vs. the map. An N-gram model is like a giant phone book — you look up an exact entry, and if it's missing, you're stuck. A neural model is like a map: even if a particular restaurant isn't marked, you can see it sits in a cluster of restaurants in one neighborhood and walk there. That “map” is the embedding space — and similar words live in the same neighborhood.

Bengio's model works in four steps. Given the previous (n−1) words, we look up each word's embedding, concatenate them into one vector x, pass x through a hidden layer with a tanh activation (from the Activation Functions — Interactive Explainer), and produce a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary with softmax (from the Softmax & Probability Outputs — Interactive Explainer):

Previous words (indices)
↓  look up in embedding matrix C
Embedding vectors → concatenate into x
↓  hidden layer + tanh
Hidden representation h
↓  output layer + softmax
Probability over the whole vocabulary

As a single formula, the probability of the next word is:

P(wt | wt-n+1, …, wt-1) = softmax(W·h + b)
where   h = tanh(H·x + d)   and   x = [ C(wt-n+1) ; … ; C(wt-1) ]

So the pre-softmax score is simply y = W·h + b — the hidden vector h passed through one more linear layer. Here C is the embedding matrix, H and d are the hidden layer's weights and bias, and W and b are the output layer's.

The crucial difference from N-grams: every parameter is learned — the embeddings C, the hidden weights H, and the output weights W are all trained jointly by showing the network millions of sentences. An N-gram model is a phone book (find the exact entry or fail); a neural model understands the structure — like knowing that numbers sharing an area code are probably the same city, even for a number you've never seen. It generalizes because it learned patterns, not entries.

The Power of Shared Representations

Here is why Bengio's idea is a game-changer. During training, the model sees “the cat sat”, “the dog sat”, “the cat ran”, “the dog ran” — so “cat” and “dog” appear in similar contexts, and their embedding vectors end up close together in space. Anything the model learns about “cat” automatically transfers to “dog”.

Animals cluster together (top-left); food clusters apart (bottom-right).

The numbers tell the story: cat–dog ≈ 0.05 (almost the same point) while cat–pizza ≈ 1.28 (far apart). The network sees cat and dog as nearly interchangeable, but cat and pizza as completely different — exactly what we want.

This solves the sparsity problem. If the model has seen “the cat sat on the mat” many times but never “the dog sat on the rug”, it can still assign that sentence a reasonable probability — because “dog” is near “cat” and “rug” is near “mat”. An N-gram model would call it impossible (probability zero); the neural model recognizes the same underlying pattern — an animal sitting on a surface.

Word2Vec and the Geometry of Meaning

In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google introduced Word2Vec — a very efficient way to train word embeddings on massive corpora. It came in two flavors: Skip-gram (predict the surrounding context words from a center word) and CBOW — Continuous Bag of Words (predict the center word from its context). It could process billions of words in hours.

The astonishing result: the embedding space captures meaningful directions. The most famous example is vector arithmetic. Pick an analogy and watch the vectors combine in the space — the direction you add is the meaning being applied:

Direction added:

This isn't a trick — it's a natural consequence of learning from context. Words used in similar ways land in similar places, and the directions between them encode relationships like gender, geography, and tense. The embedding space learned grammar, semantics, and even geography — purely from predicting words in context. The two N-gram flaws are now both gone.

Recurrent Neural Networks: Unlimited Context?

Bengio's model was a leap forward, but it still uses a fixed context window — like N-grams, it only looks at the last (n−1) words. Set n=5, and it can't use anything from 6 words ago. Can we do better?

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) offer a beautiful answer: process one word at a time, carrying a hidden state ht forward that summarizes everything seen so far. At each step, the hidden state is updated from the current word and the previous hidden state:

ht = tanh( Whh·ht-1 + Wxh·xt + b )

The Catch: Vanishing Gradients

In theory, unlimited memory. In practice, a serious problem. Training uses backpropagation through time, and the gradient flowing back to early words involves multiplying many weight matrices together. If those weights are less than 1, the gradient shrinks exponentially — the vanishing gradient problem (the same effect you saw with saturating activations in the Activation Functions — Interactive Explainer). In practice, RNNs struggle to remember beyond about 10–20 steps.

An RNN unrolled through time. At each timestep, an input xt feeds the hidden state ht, which produces an output yt and passes its memory forward (orange arrows). Backward (blue, dashed) the gradient signal fades with each step — the vanishing gradient.

Consider: “The author who wrote the bestselling series of fantasy novels that were adapted into blockbuster films was born in England.” The subject “author” and verb “was” are 15 words apart. To choose “was” over “were”, the model must remember the subject is singular — but the intervening words keep overwriting the hidden state. It's a game of telephone: the original signal is diluted by the end.

LSTMs and GRUs added learned gates to control what to remember and forget, extending effective memory to roughly 100–200 tokens — much better, but still limited.

The deeper problem: no parallelism. RNNs process words sequentially — you can't compute word 5 until words 1–4 are done. This means they can't exploit modern parallel hardware (GPUs), making them painfully slow: training a large RNN on a billion-word corpus could take weeks, since each word waits for the previous one. This sequential bottleneck is the key obstacle to scaling up.
Neural language models solved the N-gram problems by learning representations: embeddings put similar words close together, so knowledge transfers and unseen sentences get sensible probabilities. RNNs extended context but hit two walls — vanishing gradients limit memory, and sequential processing prevents parallelism. What we need is a model that can look at all words simultaneously and decide which ones matter most. That idea — attention — is the third and final era, and the subject of the next explainer in this series.
