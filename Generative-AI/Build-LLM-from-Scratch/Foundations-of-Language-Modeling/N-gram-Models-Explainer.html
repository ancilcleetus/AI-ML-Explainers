Here is the key insight: you are already a language model. Your brain, trained on decades of reading and conversation, assigns probabilities to the next word based on the words that came before. The central question of this explainer is simply: how do we teach a machine to do the same thing?

First, researchers tried counting (N-grams). Then they tried learning (neural language models). Finally, they tried attending (Transformers). Together these form the foundation on which GPT, BERT, LLaMA, and every modern large language model is built.

This explainer covers the first era — counting. It's the natural place to begin, because understanding exactly why counting eventually fails is what motivates everything that follows. The next two explainers in this series pick up with learning and attending.

Predict by Counting: The Core Idea

Think about it intuitively. Suppose you have a friend who always talks about food. You've heard them say “I want pizza” about 50 times, “I want pasta” about 30 times, and “I want sushi” about 20 times. If they begin with “I want”, what comes next? Probably pizza — because you've counted how often each word follows “I want” in your experience.

That's the whole idea behind N-gram language models: count how often words appear together, and use those counts to estimate probabilities. Formally, a language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words. Given a sentence w1, w2, …, wn, we want P(w1, w2, …, wn). Using the chain rule of probability, we decompose this joint probability into a product of conditional probabilities:

P(w1, …, wn) = P(w1) · P(w2|w1) · P(w3|w1,w2) · … · P(wn|w1,…,wn-1)

Let's see it on a real sentence: “I want pizza now”. Suppose a large corpus gives us these probabilities. Click Multiply next term to build up the product one factor at a time — the running result is computed for you:

The sentence “I want pizza now” gets a probability of 0.012% — small, but positive. A nonsensical sentence like “I want pizza elephant” would score even lower. That's exactly what we want: the model assigns higher probability to sentences that make sense.

The Markov Assumption: Short Memory

There's a problem with that last term. To estimate P(“now” | “I want pizza”), we'd count how often the exact sequence “I want pizza” appeared, then how often “now” followed it. Manageable for short sentences — but for a 20-word sentence we'd condition on a 19-word history we've almost certainly never seen.

The fix is the Markov assumption: instead of the entire history, look only at the last (n−1) words. This gives the N-gram model. A bigram (n=2) looks back one word; a trigram (n=3) looks back two:

bigram:   P(wi | w1,…,wi-1) ≈ P(wi | wi-1)
trigram:   P(wi | w1,…,wi-1) ≈ P(wi | wi-2, wi-1)

Here's what the “sliding window” of context looks like. Switch between unigram, bigram, and trigram, and step the window across the sentence — the blue words are the context the model is allowed to use, the red word is the one being predicted:

And the bigram probability is just counting: P(w2 | w1) = Count(w1, w2) ÷ Count(w1). Let's work a concrete example. Our entire training corpus is these three sentences. Pick a word pair and watch the model count occurrences and compute the probability live:

Here's the same bigram counting in Python — nothing more than dictionaries of counts:

from collections import defaultdict # Our tiny corpus corpus = [ "the cat sat on the mat", "the cat ate the fish", "the dog sat on the mat" ] # Count bigrams and unigrams bigram_counts = defaultdict(int) unigram_counts = defaultdict(int) for sentence in corpus: words = sentence.split() for i in range(len(words)): unigram_counts[words[i]] += 1 if i < len(words) - 1: bigram_counts[(words[i], words[i+1])] += 1 # Compute bigram probability def bigram_prob(w1, w2): return bigram_counts[(w1, w2)] / unigram_counts[w1] print(f"P('sat' | 'cat') = {bigram_prob('cat', 'sat')}") # 0.5 print(f"P('ate' | 'cat') = {bigram_prob('cat', 'ate')}") # 0.5 print(f"P('sat' | 'dog') = {bigram_prob('dog', 'sat')}") # 1.0

Where N-grams Break: The Sparsity Problem

N-gram models are elegant — but they have a fundamental flaw. What happens with a word pair we've never seen? Ask for P(“ran” | “cat”) in our corpus: “cat” was never followed by “ran”, so Count(“cat”, “ran”) = 0, and the probability is zero. But “the cat ran” is a perfectly valid sentence! A zero means the model thinks it's impossible — clearly wrong.

It gets dramatically worse as n grows. Pick a vocabulary size and watch the number of possible combinations explode:

Possible bigrams (V²)— Possible trigrams (V³)— Words in all of English Wikipedia~4 billion Trigram coverage—

Researchers patched this with smoothing — redistributing some probability to unseen N-grams. The simplest is Laplace (add-one) smoothing: add 1 to every count. With vocabulary size V:

Count(w1, w2) + 1Count(w1) + V

Toggle smoothing on the “ran | cat” case (our corpus has V=6: the, cat, dog, sat, ate, ran). Watch the impossible zero become a real probability — and watch the cost:

Smoothing helps, but it's a patch, not a cure. The scale of the problem is staggering: a trigram model with a 50,000-word vocabulary has 125 trillion possible trigrams, while all of English Wikipedia offers only ~4 billion words to fill them — one observation per ~31,250 slots. Most of the table is zeros, not because those sequences are impossible, but because we simply haven't seen them. No amount of smoothing conjures genuine understanding from absence.

The Deeper Problem: No Notion of Similarity

Sparsity is bad — but the most fundamental limitation of N-grams runs deeper: they have no notion of similarity. To an N-gram model, “cat” and “dog” are completely unrelated symbols — just different row indices in a table. Learning that “the cat sat on the mat” tells the model absolutely nothing about “the dog sat on the rug.” There's no understanding that cats and dogs are both animals, or that mats and rugs are both things you sit on.

What the model learned
“the cat sat on the mat”  ✓ seen
What it knows about this
“the dog sat on the rug”  ✗ nothing

Every word is an arbitrary index. No knowledge transfers between similar words.

Think of learning a foreign language. Someone teaches you “le chat dort” means “the cat sleeps.” An N-gram model is like memorizing that exact phrase while gaining zero insight about “le chien dort” (“the dog sleeps”) — because it treats “chat” and “chien” as unrelated symbols. A human reasons: “chat and chien are both animals, so the sentence probably works the same way.” N-gram models cannot. Every word combination must be observed independently.

So we're left with two deep problems: sparsity (most word combinations are never seen) and no similarity (words are arbitrary symbols with no shared meaning). Both point to the same missing ingredient — a way to represent what words actually mean, so that similar words behave similarly. That single idea — learning representations instead of counting symbols — launches the second era of language modeling, and it's where the next explainer in this series begins.
