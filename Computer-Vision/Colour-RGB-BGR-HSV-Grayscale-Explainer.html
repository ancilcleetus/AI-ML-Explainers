From one number per pixel to three

In the What Is an Image? — Interactive Explainer, you clicked on individual pixels of a kitten photo and saw their grayscale intensity values — single numbers between 0 and 255. To keep the picture simple, we treated the image as grayscale — one number per pixel.

Real photos carry colour. That means each pixel actually carries three numbers: one for red, one for green, one for blue. Click on the grid below to see this directly — same kitten, same 16×16 region, but now each cell shows its full RGB triple.

Each pixel is now described by an (R, G, B) triple — a small but crucial mental shift. The previous explainer's "grid of numbers" is now a grid of triples, equivalent to three stacked grids (one per colour channel). Everything else from the previous explainer still applies — coordinate systems, resolution, byte storage — just with the channel count multiplied in.

The byte impact: the previous explainer's 4032 × 3024 photo at 1 byte per pixel was ~12 MB uncompressed in grayscale. In full RGB colour, the same photo is 3× larger — roughly 35 MB uncompressed per photo, which matches the storage-calculator example you ran at the end of the previous explainer. The storage formula from before now reads bytes = height × width × 3 × bytes_per_channel for RGB images.

Why three channels? The biology

Why exactly three numbers per pixel, not two or seven? The answer is biological: the human eye has three types of cone cells, each sensitive to a different range of light wavelengths.

L-cones respond most strongly to long wavelengths (peaking in the red/yellow range)
M-cones respond most strongly to medium wavelengths (peaking in the green range)
S-cones respond most strongly to short wavelengths (peaking in the blue/violet range)

Your brain receives three intensity signals from these three cone types, and from that triple of signals it reconstructs your perception of all the colours you can see. Cameras and screens mimic this. Cameras capture three intensities at each pixel (using red, green, and blue filters); screens emit three intensities at each pixel (using red, green, and blue sub-pixels). The hardware on both ends maps to the biology.

Three 8-bit channels gives us 2⁸ × 2⁸ × 2⁸ = 256³ ≈ 16.8 million representable colours. That's far more than human eyes can reliably distinguish (somewhere between 1 and 10 million depending on conditions), which is why 8 bits per channel is the universal default — more bits would store distinctions we couldn't see anyway.

An important nuance — additive vs subtractive

The RGB system is additive: more red + more green + more blue = brighter, eventually white. This is how light works — combine all wavelengths and you get white light. It's also why pure black is (0, 0, 0) and pure white is (255, 255, 255).

Paint and ink work the opposite way — subtractive. Mixing all pigments yields black, not white, because each pigment absorbs certain wavelengths. That's why printers use CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, blacK) instead of RGB. In digital image processing we almost always work with the additive RGB model because we're dealing with light — sensors, screens, generated images.

Across all the computer vision in this roadmap, when you see "(R, G, B)" or "3 channels" or img.shape == (H, W, 3), this is what it means: three intensity numbers per pixel, mapping to the three cone types in your eye.

Two patterns worth noticing

First: when all three sliders are equal — (0, 0, 0), (100, 100, 100), (255, 255, 255) — the result is always a shade of gray. Equal channels means no colour preference, just brightness. This connects back to the grayscale images you worked with in the previous explainer: grayscale is just "RGB where all three channels are equal."

Second: when one channel is much higher than the others, you get a dominant hue — (230, 50, 50) is dominantly red, (50, 230, 50) is dominantly green. Mixing two high channels gives you secondary colours: red + green ≈ yellow, red + blue ≈ magenta, green + blue ≈ cyan.

Normalisation — 0–255 vs 0.0–1.0

In code you'll often see RGB values expressed in two different ways:

Integer 0–255 (uint8) — the storage format. Files on disk, JPEG/PNG output, and library load functions return this.
Float 0.0–1.0 (float32) — the format for neural network input. PyTorch's transforms.ToTensor() divides by 255 and casts to float, because gradient-based optimisation works much better with small numbers near zero.

Converting between them is a division (or multiplication) by 255:

img_float = img_uint8.astype(np.float32) / 255.0 # 0–255 → 0.0–1.0 img_uint8 = (img_float * 255).clip(0, 255).astype(np.uint8) # back

You'll see this conversion happen routinely in image preprocessing pipelines.

The BGR gotcha — the most common beginner bug

Now to the most painful and confusing thing in computer vision for newcomers. Most libraries — PIL/Pillow, scikit-image, Matplotlib, PyTorch, TensorFlow — store and expect RGB-ordered pixels. The channels are (R, G, B) in that order.

But OpenCV stores images in BGR order — (B, G, R). When you load an image with cv2.imread(...), the array you get back has the red and blue channels swapped relative to what every other library uses.

This is the single most common beginner bug in computer vision. If you don't convert, here's what you see when you try to display an OpenCV-loaded image with another library:

✓ RGB displayed correctly
yellow flower, green leaves, blue sky
✗ BGR interpreted as RGB (the bug)
yellow becomes blue — instantly recognisable when you know what to look for

The yellow sunflower turns vivid blue because yellow is high-R + high-G, and after the swap it becomes high-B + high-G (which is cyan-blue). Notice the green leaves stay roughly green — because green is the middle channel and isn't swapped. You can spot this bug visually in two seconds once you've seen it: skies look orange, foliage looks fine, anything yellow becomes blue.

Why does OpenCV do this?

Historical reasons. OpenCV started in the late 1990s on Windows, where the BMP image file format stored pixels in BGR order. The library adopted the same convention to avoid swapping on load, and never changed it because too much existing code would break. Twenty-five years later, you still pay the price every time you load an image with it.

How to fix it in code

Add one line right after every cv2.imread:

import cv2 import matplotlib.pyplot as plt img_bgr = cv2.imread('photo.jpg') # OpenCV loads in BGR img_rgb = cv2.cvtColor(img_bgr, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB) # convert to RGB plt.imshow(img_rgb) # now displays correctly

Alternatively, since the issue is just channel reordering, you can do it directly with NumPy slicing — same result, no OpenCV call needed for the conversion:

img_rgb = img_bgr[:, :, ::-1] # reverse the channel axis: BGR → RGB
The rule of thumb: if you load with cv2.imread, you almost always want to convert to RGB before doing anything else with the array. The only exception is when you're passing it to another OpenCV function — in that case keep it in BGR so they stay consistent.

Alternative colour spaces — HSV and grayscale

RGB is the default storage format, but it's not always the best way to reason about colour for a specific task. Two alternatives come up often in CV: HSV and grayscale.

HSV — hue, saturation, value

HSV decomposes a colour into three more perceptually-meaningful axes:

Hue (H, 0–360°) — "what colour" on the colour wheel: 0°=red, 60°=yellow, 120°=green, 180°=cyan, 240°=blue, 300°=magenta, back to 360°=red.
Saturation (S, 0–100%) — "how vivid": 0 is fully gray, 100 is the pure hue.
Value (V, 0–100%) — "how bright": 0 is black, 100 is fully lit.

Try the HSV picker:

Hue (H) 40°
Saturation (S) 80%
Value (V) 90%

The key practical advantage of HSV: hue is robust to lighting changes. Consider trying to detect a red object that's partly in shadow. In RGB, the lit part might be (220, 50, 40) and the shadowed part (90, 30, 25) — three completely different numbers per channel. In HSV, both regions have hue ≈ 0° (red), they just differ in V (brightness). If you're building a colour-based segmentation system, HSV gives you a much more stable signal to threshold on.

You'll see HSV used for: green-screen keying in video, segmenting objects by colour (e.g. tracking a red ball in a video), skin-tone detection, and any colour-based filter that needs to ignore lighting variation.

Grayscale — discarding colour entirely

The opposite move: drop colour entirely and keep just brightness. A grayscale image is a single channel — one number per pixel — exactly like the images in the previous explainer. Why would you do this?

Classical CV algorithms operate on intensity. Edge detection (Sobel, Canny), corner detection (Harris), gradient-based feature descriptors (SIFT, HOG) — none of them care about colour. They look at how brightness changes across pixels. Working in grayscale is simpler, faster (3× less data), and often more robust.

Some recognition tasks don't need colour. Reading text from documents, detecting faces, recognising handwriting — colour doesn't help and might even hurt by adding irrelevant variability.

Compute and memory budget. When you have to process millions of images, 3× faster everywhere is hard to argue with.

How do we collapse three numbers into one? The standard formula is a weighted average:

gray = 0.299·R + 0.587·G + 0.114·B

Notice that green dominates with ~59% weight, while blue is only ~11%. That's because human eyes are far more sensitive to green than blue — most of our cones are M-cones (green), so green contributes most to our sense of brightness. The weights are calibrated to match human perception, so the resulting grayscale image looks "correctly bright" to us.

Now the difference is dramatic. Under naive average, the rose looks medium-gray. Under luminance, it's slightly darker (red is only 30% weight). Under green-only, the rose almost disappears — because pure red contains almost no green signal. Meanwhile the green leaves and foliage in the background become brighter in the green-only image — they're the only thing left. This is why the choice of grayscale conversion method actually matters for red-heavy images, and why classical CV pipelines that work on red subjects (fire detection, tomato sorting, blood-cell counting) need to use the right formula.

The luminance formula is the right choice almost always. Both OpenCV (cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)) and PIL (img.convert('L')) use it by default. The naive average and single-channel options exist mostly as "here are simpler alternatives, but use the weighted formula in practice."
