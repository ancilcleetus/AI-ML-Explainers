The one-sentence claim

Look at the photo of the kitten on the left below. To you it's a kitten. To a computer, it's a grid of numbers. That's the whole secret of computer vision — and once you see images that way you cannot unsee it.

The grid on the right is a tiny 16×16 region from the kitten's face, displayed cell-by-cell with each pixel as a single shaded square. Click any cell to see what number actually sits there.

The same image to a computer

Each cell carries a number between 0 and 255, where 0 means "completely black" and 255 means "completely white." The grid you're looking at on the right is just a small 16×16 region — only 256 numbers total. The full photo on the left has 96 × 96 = 9,216 numbers, and a typical phone photo has tens of millions.

This is the single most important idea in all of computer vision. Every algorithm in the roadmap that follows — convolution filters, CNNs, ViTs, object detectors, image generators — all of them operate on these grids of numbers. Different ways of doing things to the grid.

What a pixel actually is

A pixel is the smallest unit of an image — a single "sample" of the visual world. The number inside it represents intensity: how much light is at that point in the scene. The grid in §1 — The One-Sentence Claim showed grayscale intensities — one number per pixel. (Colour adds two more numbers per pixel; that's the topic of the next explainer, but we'll set it aside for now.)

The 0–255 convention

Why 0 to 255? Because each pixel is typically stored in 8 bits — eight binary digits — which can represent 2⁸ = 256 distinct values. The convention is that 0 means "no light" (pure black) and 255 means "maximum light" (pure white). Everything in between is some shade of gray:

Try mapping the swatch values to what you saw in §1's pixel grid. The kitten's ear (dark fur) lands around 30–60, the cheek (white fur) lands around 200–240, and the eye is somewhere in the middle. Those numbers were always there — you just had to know how to read them.

Why 8 bits?

256 distinct levels turn out to be enough that human eyes can't reliably distinguish adjacent values — the gradient from one swatch to the next above looks smooth. More bits per channel (10-bit or 12-bit, used in professional photography and HDR) capture more nuance and avoid "banding" in subtle gradients. Fewer bits per channel (1-bit, used in fax machines) loses everything between black and white. 8 bits is the universal default for everyday digital images.

The 0–255 number range you keep seeing in code — np.array([12, 88, 200]) indexing into images, img > 128 creating a mask, .astype(np.uint8) conversion — all of it comes from this single 8-bit-per-channel storage convention.

How we address individual pixels

If an image is a grid of numbers, you need a way to point at a specific cell. Two different conventions exist, and confusing them is the source of more beginner bugs than any other single thing in computer vision.

Drag the sliders. The highlighted cell is the same pixel in both grids — but the numbers describing it are swapped (and in some math textbooks, y also flips upside-down so the origin is at the bottom-left). The visual result is identical; only the language describing it changes.

Why two systems exist

The (row, col) system comes from how arrays are laid out in computer memory — rows of pixels stored top-to-bottom, columns left-to-right. NumPy, PyTorch, and pretty much every modern array library use this. Cartesian (x, y) comes from mathematics (graphs, coordinate geometry) where x is horizontal and y is vertical. OpenCV adopted (x, y) for some of its display and drawing functions to feel familiar to people coming from graphics programming, but its underlying image arrays are still row-major.

The y-axis flip bug — when you switch between libraries (NumPy ↔ OpenCV ↔ matplotlib), keep track of whether the function expects (row, col) or (x, y). Drawing a circle at (50, 100) with OpenCV's cv2.circle places it at x=50, y=100 — which is column 50, row 100 — which is at the top of a portrait image. The same coordinates passed to NumPy as img[50, 100] indexes row 50, column 100 — a completely different pixel.

This roadmap uses NumPy-style (row, col) indexing throughout unless explicitly noted otherwise. When you see img[y, x] in code, "y" means "the y-th row" — that's the convention.

Resolution — how big is the grid?

So far we've looked at small grids (16×16 in §1, 8×8 in §3). Real images are much bigger. Resolution is the size of the grid — typically written as height × width, sometimes given as a megapixel count (a million pixels).

The 224 × 224 milestone

Notice that the 224 × 224 version is the smallest size at which the kitten still looks clearly like a kitten — the ears, eyes, whiskers, and face shape are all preserved. That's why 224 × 224 became the standard input size for ImageNet-trained CNNs (ResNet, VGG, MobileNet — all the architectures you'll meet later in the roadmap). It's the smallest grid that's still semantically rich enough for classification, which means fast training and modest GPU memory.

Modern transformer-based models (ViT) sometimes use larger inputs — 384 × 384 or 512 × 512 — because attention layers are happier with more tokens. But 224 × 224 remains the default for "does this fit and train fast?"

The diminishing-returns story: doubling the resolution quadruples the pixel count (and roughly quadruples the compute and memory cost). Going from 16 × 16 to 32 × 32 is a huge visual win; from 224 × 224 to 384 × 384 the human-perceptible gain is much smaller, but the cost is the same multiplier. This is why CNN papers obsessively report "accuracy at 224 × 224" — it's the sweet spot.

Aspect ratio — making a non-square image square

The kitten in §4 was a square photo from the start. Real-world images almost never are — phone photos are 4:3 or 3:4, cameras shoot 3:2, landscapes are 16:9. But CNNs almost always expect square inputs (224 × 224, 384 × 384, etc.). Something has to give.

Try the three standard techniques on this 16:9 alpine view. Each produces a 192 × 192 square output, but they make different sacrifices.

Original 16:9 image
Which one to choose?

All three are common in practice, and the choice depends on what you're training and what your data looks like:

Squish (resize without preserving aspect ratio): simplest, preserves every pixel of input, but distorts objects. Works surprisingly well for CNN training because data augmentation makes models robust to minor distortions. Used in PyTorch's default transforms.Resize((224, 224)).

Centre-crop after resize: the standard ImageNet preprocessing recipe. Resize so the shorter side is 256, then take the centre 224 × 224. Preserves aspect ratio of objects but loses the corners. Almost every published CNN result uses this.

Letterbox (resize to fit, pad with black): preserves the entire image with no distortion. Used in object-detection systems (YOLO, RetinaNet) where you can't afford to lose any potential object on the sides. The padding adds "wasted" pixels to the network input.

The forward pointer: you'll see all three methods again when we get to data augmentation and CNN training pipelines later in the roadmap.

Storage and why compression exists

Each pixel is 1 byte for grayscale (8 bits = 1 byte), or 3 bytes for colour (R, G, B at 8 bits each). The total storage for an uncompressed image is simply:

bytes = height × width × channels × bytes_per_channel

A single iPhone photo at 12 megapixels is about 35 MB uncompressed. A 1-second 4K video at 30 fps would be over 700 MB. A 90-minute 4K movie would be over 3 TB. Storage and transmission would be impossible without compression.

Why image compression had to be invented

Two main approaches make image files practical:

Lossy compression (JPEG, 1992): exploits the fact that human eyes don't perceive every detail equally. JPEG discards high-frequency information that we don't consciously notice, compressing photos 10–20× with imperceptible quality loss at normal viewing sizes. The trade-off is that every save degrades the image slightly — "JPEG of a JPEG of a JPEG" gets progressively worse.

Lossless compression (PNG, 1996): uses redundancy in the image — large solid regions, repeated patterns — to compress without losing any pixel value. Smaller than uncompressed but bigger than JPEG. Perfect for screenshots, icons, anything with sharp edges or text.

When we get to the Loading, Displaying, and Saving Images — Interactive Explainer later in this category, you'll see what "JPEG quality 90" actually means at the byte level, and you'll compare the same photo at different compression settings.

Compression is what makes the internet visual. Every photo on every website, every webcam feed, every video streaming service — they all rely on aggressive compression. The 35 MB iPhone photo you just computed ends up around 2–4 MB after JPEG compression, and the difference is invisible to your eyes.

The road ahead

You now know what an image is: a grid of numbers, each between 0 and 255, addressed by (row, col), with the grid size called resolution and the total byte cost determined by simple multiplication. Everything else in this roadmap is a different thing to do with that grid.
